Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'An electron jumps from n=4 to n=2 in a hydrogen-like atom (or ion with Z=…); find the energy, wavelength or frequency of the photon.'

• 'The energy of the photon is 2.86 eV (or the wavelength is … nm); find the final level, the ion or Z.'

• 'A hydrogen atom is excited to level n (or bombarded by 12.5 eV electrons): how many spectral lines are emitted?' and Franck–Hertz.

2. Formulae and laws

Energy of a level

En=−13.6Z2n2eV

Photon energy of a transition

E=13.6Z2(1n12−1n22)eV

Wavelength from energy

λ(nm)=1240E(eV), λ(Å)=12400E(eV)

Frequency of the photon

f=Eh, h=4.14×10−15eV s

Number of spectral lines from level n

N=n(n−1)2

First excitation energy of hydrogen

13.6−3.4=10.2 eV; ionisation energy 13.6 eV

Minimum energy to emit a Balmer line

E3−E1=12.09 eV (the atom must reach n=3)

Franck–Hertz energy loss

energy lost by the electron = gap between two levels (here 5.6−0.7=4.9 eV)

3. Concepts and skills

Which transition gives the shortest wavelength?

The one with the largest energy gap; in a figure choose the longest arrow.

Can an atom absorb any photon energy?

No. A photon is absorbed only if its energy equals a gap between two levels. An electron can give up part of its energy.

How is the number of lines counted when an atom is excited to n?

Count every possible jump between the levels up to n: n(n−1)/2. An atom that stops at n=2 gives only 1 line.

Why is 10.2 eV enough for only one line?

It lifts the electron to n=2 only, and the only jump back is 2→1.

What happens to an ion such as Li++?

The levels scale with Z2: multiply the hydrogen energies by 9 for Z=3.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using Z=1 for an ion, or forgetting Z2.

• Counting the 'first excited state' as n=1; it is n=2 (third excited state is n=4).

• Counting lines as n−1 instead of n(n−1)/2.

• Using hc=1240 eV nm with the energy in joules or the wavelength in metres.

• Assuming an electron beam of 12.5 eV excites only one level.

rn=0.529n2ZÅ

Speed of the electron

vn=2.19×106Znm s−1

Time period and frequency

T∝n3Z2, f∝Z2n3

Energy of the electron

En=−13.6Z2n2 eV, K=−E, U=2E

L=mvr=nh2π, so L∝n∝r

3. Concepts and skills

Why does the speed fall as n grows?

v∝1/n: a larger orbit means a weaker attraction and a slower electron.

What is the ratio of kinetic to potential energy in any orbit?

K/U=−1/2; the magnitude is 1/2 whatever n is.

When do two ions have the same energy?

When Z/n is the same, for example He+ at n=2 and hydrogen at n=1.

What happens to K, U and E when the electron moves to a higher orbit?

K decreases, U and E increase (they become less negative).

Why is L quantised?

Bohr's postulate: mvr=nh/2π; the change of L is (Δn)h/2π.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Writing r∝n instead of n2, or forgetting the factor 1/Z.

• Taking the ground state as n=0.

• Treating the 'second orbit' as L when L is the ground-state value: the change is then L, not 2L.

• Giving K=+E (it is K=−E).

• Missing the factor 1/T in the current I=ev/2πr.

3. Concepts and skills

Which series is visible?

Balmer (n1=2): Hα is red, Hβ blue-green.

Which member of a series has the longest wavelength?

The first member (n2=n1+1); the lines crowd towards the shortest wavelength at the limit.

Why do Lyman lines lie in the ultraviolet?

They end in the ground state and carry the largest gaps (10.2 to 13.6 eV).

How do you find a missing line from known ones?

Add the energies of the two jumps, or add the wave numbers 1/λ, never the wavelengths.

Do R and hc matter in a ratio?

No, they cancel; only the brackets (1n12−1n22) remain.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Taking the second member of a series as the first, or counting members from n1 instead of from n1+1.

• Adding wavelengths of two steps instead of adding 1/λ.

• Inverting a ratio of wavelengths and getting the ratio of energies.

• Using the series limit (n2=∞) for the longest wavelength.

• Forgetting Z2 for a hydrogen-like ion.

3. Concepts and skills

What did the gold-foil experiment show?

Most α-particles pass straight through and a few scatter at large angles: the positive charge and mass sit in a tiny nucleus.

Why does a classical Rutherford atom collapse?

An accelerating electron radiates, loses energy and spirals into the nucleus.

What is the nuclear density?

About 2.3×1017 kg m​−3, independent of the mass number.

Why is Bohr's model limited to hydrogen-like atoms?

It ignores the repulsion between electrons, so it works only for one-electron systems.

What does the Thomson model predict?

A sphere of positive charge with embedded electrons and no tiny dense core; it cannot give large-angle scattering.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using b∝tan(θ/2) instead of cot(θ/2).

• Using the charge of one proton for the α-particle (it is 2e) in the closest approach.

• Picking an increasing curve for the scattering graph.

• Thinking nuclear density grows with the mass number.

• Reading hf=E1−E2 with the wrong sign for absorption and emission.

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'A particle of mass m moves in a central potential U=U0r4 (or 1/2kr2); apply Bohr's quantisation.'

• 'The electron is replaced by a muon' (mass 207me) and the ionisation potential is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Bohr condition with any force

mvr=nh2π and mv2r=F(r)=−dUdr

Power-law potential U=U0r4

mv2=4U0r4⇒rn∝n1/3

Potential U=1/2kr2

mv2=kr2⇒rn∝n, E=K+U=kr2∝n

Muonic hydrogen

E∝m: ionisation energy =207×13.6 eV =2815 eV

Kα X-ray

EKα=EK−EL=hcλ

Photoelectron in a magnetic field

r=mveB, K=(eBr)22m, ϕ=hf−K

3. Concepts and skills

How is a new Bohr problem solved?

Equate the force to mv2/r, then use mvr=nh/2π to eliminate v and find rn; add K and U for En.

Why does a muon bind more strongly?

The binding energy scales with the particle's mass, so a muon 207 times heavier gives 207 times the energy.

What is the energy of an atom with a hole in the K shell?

The atom's energy is higher by the energy needed to remove that electron; the Kα photon is the difference between the K-hole and L-hole energies.

How do you combine two topics in one question?

First find the photon energy from the Bohr transition, then use K=hf−ϕ and the circular path.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using r∝n2 for a non-Coulomb force.

• Forgetting that E=K+U in a power-law potential.

• Taking the energy of the L-hole state as the energy of the Kα photon.

• Using the electron mass for a muon.

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'The recoil speed of a hydrogen atom after it emits a photon in going from n=5 to n=1.'

• 'Due to recoil, the percentage change in the wavelength of the emitted light is…'

2. Formulae and laws

Photon momentum

p=Ec=hλ

Recoil speed of the atom

v=EMc, with M≈1.67×10−27 kg for hydrogen

Recoil energy

K=p22M=E22Mc2

Fractional change in the wavelength

Δλλ≈E2Mc2∼10−9

3. Concepts and skills

Why does the atom recoil?

Momentum is conserved: the photon carries momentum E/c and the atom takes the opposite momentum.

Is the recoil energy important?

No: Erecoil/E≈10−9, so the photon energy is almost unchanged.

Which mass do you use?

The mass of the whole atom (1.6×10−27 kg for hydrogen), not the electron mass.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the electron mass instead of the mass of the atom.

• Forgetting to convert the photon energy from eV to J before dividing by c.

• Expecting a large change in wavelength from the recoil.
