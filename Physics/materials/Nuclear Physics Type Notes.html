Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'A radioactive substance falls to 1/8 (or 7/8 has decayed, or 6.25% is left) in …; find the half-life or the time.'

• 'The activity falls from 6.4×10−4 Ci to 5×10−6 Ci (or the count rate from 1600 to 100 in 8 s); find the half-life, the decay constant or the rate at another time.'

• 'Find the activity of 1.5 mg of ​198Au (or of 1μg of a substance with decay constant 1.5×10−5s−1).'

• 'A capacitor discharges through R while a sample decays: the ratio of charge to activity stays fixed.'

2. Formulae and laws

Number of half-lives

n=tT

Nuclei or activity left

N=N0(12)t/T=N0e−λt, A=A0e−λt

Fraction decayed

1−(12)n

Half-life and mean life

T=ln2λ=0.693τ, τ=1λ

Decay constant from two readings

λ=1tlnA1A2

Activity of a mass m

A=λN=λmMNA, 1Ci=3.7×1010Bq

Discharging capacitor

q=q0e−t/RC, so RC=τ when q/A is constant

3. Concepts and skills

Can I use the activity in place of the number of nuclei?

Yes. A=λN is proportional to N, so it falls by the same factor.

What does '7/8 has decayed' mean?

1/8 is left: three half-lives.

What if the ratio is not a power of 2?

Use λt=ln(ratio) with ln2=0.693, for example ln1.4=0.336.

What is the mean life?

The average life of a nucleus, τ=1/λ=1.44T; after one mean life N=N0/e (about 37 %).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Reading '1/8 decayed' as '1/8 left', or the reverse.

• Not converting minutes, days or years to seconds before using λ in A=λN.

• Using τ instead of T in N0(1/2)t/T.

• Forgetting that 33% decayed means 67% left, so the interval from 33% to 67% decay is one half-life.

• Using the mass of the sample as the number of nuclei (divide by the molar mass and multiply by NA).

2. Formulae and laws

Mass defect

Δm=Zmp+(A−Z)mn−mnucleus

Binding energy

BE=Δmc2=Δm×931.5MeV (with Δm in u)

Energy released from BE per nucleon b

Q=∑Abproducts−∑Abreactants

Q-value from masses

Q=(∑mreactants−∑mproducts)×931.5MeV

Energy of 1 g

E=mc2=9×1013J=5.6×1026MeV

Number of atoms in a mass

N=mMNA

Unit conversions

1MeV=1.6×10−13J, 1kWh=3.6×106J

Reaction rate from power

rate=PQ

3. Concepts and skills

When is energy released?

When the products have more total binding energy than the reactants (that is, a smaller total mass).

Why must I multiply the binding energy per nucleon by A?

The table gives the energy per nucleon; the total for each nucleus is Ab.

Which masses do I use for Q?

Atomic masses on both sides (the electron masses cancel) or nuclear masses on both sides; do not mix them.

Why does fusion give more energy per kg than fission?

Fusion gives about 6.7 MeV per nucleon (26.7 MeV for four H) and fission about 0.85 MeV per nucleon (200 MeV for 235).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Writing the mass defect in u and forgetting ×931.5 MeV.

• Using the binding energy per nucleon without multiplying by the mass number.

• Counting one fission only for a whole sample (use N=mNA/M).

• Mixing J, MeV and kWh.

• In the triple alpha process: forgetting that each reaction uses three helium nuclei.

Ratio of two samples

Fraction left with half-lives

Mean life and half-life

τ=1λ, T=0.693λ

Atoms in a mass

Two processes in one sample

λ=λ1+λ2, 1T=1T1+1T2

Effective half-life

3. Concepts and skills

Which decay constants do I subtract?

For two separate samples, take e−λAt/e−λBt; the exponents subtract.

Two processes in one nucleus: why do the constants add?

The nucleus decays by either route, so the probabilities per second add: λ=λ1+λ2.

Is the effective half-life longer or shorter than either?

Shorter than both. A much faster process decides it (10 s and 100 s give 9.1 s).

Do the atomic weights matter?

Only when you count atoms. A ratio of masses left does not depend on them.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Adding the half-lives instead of the decay constants.

• Taking T=τ (the mean life is 1.44T).

• Comparing activities when numbers of nuclei are asked, and the reverse.

• Taking the fraction left instead of the fraction decayed (63/64 against 1/64).

• Using the atomic weights when only masses are asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Nuclear radius

R=R0A1/3, R0=1.2–1.3 fm

Volume

V=4/3πR03A∝A

Radius ratio

R1R2=(A1A2)1/3

Nuclear density

ρ=3mn4πR03≈2×1017kg m−3 (same for all nuclei)

Graph

lnRR0=13lnA, a straight line through the origin

3. Concepts and skills

Does the density change with A?

No. The mass is proportional to A and so is the volume, so every nucleus has the same density.

How large is the nuclear density?

About 1017 kg m​−3, roughly 1014 times the density of water.

Which is bigger, a copper or a carbon nucleus?

Copper has the larger radius, but the density is the same.

What does the straight-line graph of lnR against lnA show?

The slope is 1/3 because R∝A1/3.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Saying that heavier nuclei are denser.

• Using R∝A instead of A1/3 (a radius half as large means A is 1/8).

• Cubing the wrong ratio.

• Leaving R0 in fm when the mass is in kg (convert 1fm=10−15 m).

• Writing the density of water as 1 kg m​−3 instead of 103 kg m​−3.

γ emission

no change in A or Z

3. Concepts and skills

Which particle changes A?

Only the α particle: use it first to fix the number of α emissions.

Is the half-life shorter on the steeper line of a lnR–t graph?

Yes. The slope is the decay constant.

What does the intermediate nucleus do in P→Q→R?

Q starts from zero, reaches a maximum and falls; P falls all the time and the stable R only grows.

What does the intercept of the lnR graph give?

The initial rate, lnR0 at t=0.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Treating β+ as β− (Z goes down, not up).

• Counting γ emissions as a change in A or Z.

• Using a graph of N itself when only lnN is a straight line.

• Reading the slope from the wrong axis scale.

• Choosing a graph in which the intermediate nucleus does not start from zero.

3. Concepts and skills

Why is one photon not enough in e++e−→γ?

One photon cannot carry away the momentum; two photons are needed.

Which product takes more of Q?

The lighter one: the momenta are equal, so K=p2/2m is larger for the smaller mass.

What is the sign of Q for d+d→​3He+n and for p+t→d+d?

Positive for the first (energy released), negative for the second (energy needed).

When is a reaction allowed?

When energy and momentum are conserved together with charge, baryon number and lepton number, and the initial rest mass is larger than the final rest mass.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Giving all of Q to the α particle (it gets Q(A−4)/A).

• Balancing A but not Z, or forgetting the neutrons in fission.

• Ignoring the electron mass when comparing masses in a p→n or e+e− question.

• Believing that Q>0 is needed for every reaction (only Kp+Q>0).

• Forgetting the neutrino or antineutrino in a beta process.

2. Formulae and laws

Binding energy per nucleon

b≈8 MeV, almost constant for 30<A<170

Beta decay of the neutron

n→p+e−+ν‾

Beta-plus decay (inside a nucleus)

p→n+e++ν

Decay law

lnN=lnN0−λt, so the slope is −λ=−1τ

Half-life and decay constant

λT=0.693 (constant)

Packing fraction

M−AA: smaller means more stable

3. Concepts and skills

Why is the binding energy per nucleon constant?

The nuclear force is short ranged and saturates: a nucleon interacts only with its neighbours.

Is the nuclear force central?

No. It depends on spin, has a non-central part, is charge independent and does not follow the inverse square law.

Can temperature or chemical state change radioactivity?

No. It is random, spontaneous and independent of physical and chemical conditions.

Why does a free neutron decay but not a free proton?

The neutron is heavier than the proton; the reverse process would need energy that a free proton does not have.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Calling the nuclear force long ranged.

• Writing e+ in the decay of the neutron, or the neutrino in place of the antineutrino.

• Thinking the half-life depends on temperature or on the amount of substance.

• Taking the free proton as unstable and the free neutron as stable (it is the other way round).

• Forgetting the sign: the slope of lnN against t is −λ.
