Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• The question asks 'how many isomers', 'how many stereoisomers' or 'which shows geometrical (optical) isomerism'.

• A Newman projection or a name such as eclipsed, staggered, gauche, anti, skew is given and the stability or dihedral angle is asked.

• The structure has a carbonyl group and the 'enol content' or tautomer is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Stereoisomers with n different chiral centres

2n (fewer if the molecule has a plane of symmetry: meso forms)

Geometrical isomers of R−CH=CH−R′

2 (cis and trans) if R≠R′ at each end carbon

Optical purity

observed rotationspecific rotation of the pure isomer×100

Dihedral angles in ethane

staggered 60∘ (most stable); eclipsed 0∘ (least stable)

Stability order of n-butane conformers

anti > gauche > partially eclipsed > fully eclipsed

Enol content

highest in β-diketones (hydrogen bonding and conjugation): CH3COCH2COCH3

3. Concepts and skills

How do I count structural isomers without missing any?

Start with the straight chain, then shorten it by one carbon and place the branch at every non-equivalent position; repeat for each shorter chain.

When is a molecule chiral?

When it has no plane or centre of symmetry; the usual sign is a carbon attached to four different groups.

Why do some molecules with chiral centres have fewer than 2n stereoisomers?

If the two ends of the molecule are identical, a meso form appears and the number drops (for example 1,2-dimethylcyclopropane has 3, not 4).

What decides the stability of a conformation?

Torsional strain (eclipsing) and steric strain (bulky groups close together); there is no angle strain in an acyclic compound.

What do metamerism and functional group isomerism mean?

Metamers differ in the groups on either side of a functional atom (ethers, ketones); functional group isomers have different functional groups (alcohol and ether of C2H6O).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Counting a carbon with two identical groups as a chiral centre.

• Forgetting that a double bond shows geometrical isomerism only if both ends have two different groups.

• Counting an enantiomer pair twice, or missing the meso form.

• Missing isomers that differ only in the position of the double bond or substituent.

1. How do I spot this type?

• A structure is drawn and 'the IUPAC name is' is asked, or a name is given and the structure has to be picked from four pictures.

2. Formulae and laws

Bonds

single =1σ; double =1σ+1π; triple =1σ+2π

Hybridisation

4 electron regions: sp3; 3 regions: sp2; 2 regions: sp

Priority of functional groups (high to low)

−COOH>−SO3H>−COOR>−COCl>−CONH2>−CN>−CHO>>C=O>−OH>−NH2>C=C>C≡C

Prefixes only (never the suffix)

−Br,−Cl,−NO2,−OR,−R

Alphabetical order

substituents are alphabetised ignoring di, tri, tetra

Carbon classes

1∘, 2∘, 3∘ by the number of carbons attached to the carbon

3. Concepts and skills

What are the steps to name a compound?

Find the principal functional group, the longest chain that contains it, number from the end that gives it the lowest locant, then add substituents alphabetically.

How do I choose between two numbering directions?

First give the lowest locants to the principal group, then to multiple bonds, then to substituents together, then to the one first in alphabetical order.

What is the trick for σ and π bond counting questions?

Draw the full structure; count all bonds, then count one π for each double bond and two for each triple bond.

How do I decide hybridisation quickly?

Count sigma bonds plus lone pairs on the atom; or use the bond types: all single bonds is sp3, one double bond is sp2, one triple or two double bonds is sp.

Which compounds are 'benzenoid'?

Those containing a benzene ring; aromatic compounds without a benzene ring (like furan, thiophene) are non-benzenoid.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Numbering from the wrong end so that the functional group does not get the lowest locant.

• Choosing the longest chain when a chain that contains the functional group is available.

• Counting only σ or only π bonds when the total is asked.

• Treating −CHO as a substituent (formyl) when it can be the principal group.

1. How do I spot this type?

• A mixture and a separation technique have to be matched, or the best method for a given mixture is asked.

• A chromatogram is drawn with spots at marked distances and Rf or the order of elution is asked.

• A statement about the principle of a technique (differential adsorption, partition, solubility) is to be judged.

2. Formulae and laws

Retardation factor

Rf=distance moved by the compounddistance moved by the solvent front

Range of Rf

always less than 1 and has no units

Order of elution in a column

less polar compounds are adsorbed less and come out first

Steam distillation

for steam-volatile, water-immiscible compounds (aniline, essential oils)

Reduced pressure distillation

for liquids that decompose near their boiling point (glycerol)

Fractional distillation

for liquids with a small difference in boiling points (below about 30K)

3. Concepts and skills

How do I choose the purification method?

Match the property: boiling point difference (distillation), decomposition (reduced pressure), steam volatility (steam distillation), solubility (crystallisation, extraction), sublimation (naphthalene, camphor).

How do I read a TLC plate?

Measure from the baseline to the centre of each spot and to the solvent front; the more polar compound travels less and has the lower Rf.

What principle does column chromatography use?

Differential adsorption on silica gel or alumina; paper chromatography uses partition.

How is differential extraction done?

By shaking with an immiscible solvent in which the compound is more soluble; acidic, basic and neutral compounds are separated by adjusting the pH.

Why is a solid sublimed?

When it passes directly to vapour on heating and the impurities do not.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Measuring Rf from the bottom of the plate instead of from the baseline.

• Using simple distillation for liquids with close boiling points.

• Taking Rf greater than 1 or giving a unit.

• Confusing adsorption (surface) with absorption.

3. Concepts and skills

How do I solve a Dumas question?

Convert the volume of N2 to STP, find the mass of nitrogen from 28/22400 g per mL, and divide by the mass of the compound.

Why does Kjeldahl's method fail for some compounds?

Nitrogen in nitro, azo compounds and ring nitrogen (pyridine) is not converted to ammonium sulphate.

What is the role of CuSO4 and K2SO4 in Kjeldahl's method?

CuSO4 is a catalyst; K2SO4 raises the boiling point of the acid.

How is sulphur estimated in the Carius method?

Fuming HNO3 oxidises sulphur to H2SO4, which is precipitated as BaSO4.

How do I avoid unit slips?

Keep the mass in grams and the volume in mL, and check that the answer is below 100%.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Forgetting to convert the gas volume to STP in Dumas problems.

• Using molarity instead of normality for H2SO4 in Kjeldahl problems.

• Using the molar mass of AgX instead of the atomic mass of X (or the reverse).

• Mixing mg and g, giving a percentage above 100.

1. How do I spot this type?

• Compounds are drawn and the 'correct order of acidity' (or pKa) is asked.

• A marked hydrogen (a, b, c) has to be arranged in order of acidity.

• Amines, anilines or conjugate bases are to be arranged in order of basicity or pKb.

2. Formulae and laws

Acid strength

stronger acid ⇔ more stable conjugate base ⇔ lower pKa

pKa

pKa=−logKa

Acidity of C-H by hybridisation

sp>sp2>sp3 (HC≡CH>H2C=CH2>CH3CH3)

Carboxylic acids

F3CCOOH>Cl3CCOOH>ClCH2COOH>HCOOH>CH3COOH

Phenol versus alcohol

C6H5OH>ROH (phenoxide is resonance stabilised)

Basicity of amines (gas phase)

3∘>2∘>1∘>NH3; aniline is weaker than NH3 (lone pair delocalised)

3. Concepts and skills

How do I compare acidities quickly?

Remove the proton, draw the conjugate base, and rank the bases by stability: electron-withdrawing groups, resonance and more s-character stabilise it.

Why is a nitro-substituted phenol more acidic?

The −NO2 group at the ortho or para position delocalises the negative charge by −M and −I effects.

What makes an amine more basic?

Electron-donating groups on nitrogen increase the availability of the lone pair; resonance or electron-withdrawing groups reduce it.

How are α-hydrogens ranked?

By the stability of the enolate: two carbonyl groups (acetylacetone) are the most acidic, then one carbonyl, then ordinary C-H.

What is the relation between pKa and pKb of a conjugate pair?

pKa+pKb=14 at 25∘C.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Ranking by Ka when pKa is asked (the order reverses).

• Forgetting that +I groups lower acidity but raise basicity.

• Comparing the acids instead of their conjugate bases.

• Ignoring resonance when comparing phenols and alcohols.

1. How do I spot this type?

• Several cyclic structures are drawn and 'which are aromatic' or 'how many aromatic compounds' is asked.

• Resonance structures are drawn and their relative stability or validity is asked.

• Reactivity towards electrophilic or nucleophilic substitution is to be ranked.

2. Formulae and laws

Hückel's rule

cyclic, planar, fully conjugated, with (4n+2)π electrons (2,6,10,…)

Anti-aromatic

cyclic, planar, conjugated with 4nπ electrons (4,8,…)

Common aromatic ions

cyclopentadienyl anion (6π), cycloheptatrienyl cation (6π), cyclopropenyl cation (2π)

Resonance energy

actual structure is more stable than the most stable contributor by the resonance energy

Activating groups for EAS

−OH,−NH2,−OR,−R increase the rate; −NO2,−CN,−CHO decrease it

Nucleophilic aromatic substitution

fastest with −NO2 at ortho/para positions

3. Concepts and skills

How do I check aromaticity step by step?

Is the ring cyclic? planar (no sp3 carbon in the ring)? every ring atom has a p orbital? count the π electrons: 4n+2 is aromatic.

Do lone pairs count as π electrons?

Yes, if the lone pair is in a p orbital of a ring atom (pyrrole, furan); a lone pair in an sp2 orbital in the plane (pyridine nitrogen) does not.

Which resonance structure is most stable?

One with the most covalent bonds, full octets, negative charge on the more electronegative atom, and the least separation of charge.

Why does a carbocation next to a benzene ring react faster?

Resonance with the ring delocalises the charge, which stabilises the cation.

How are rates of electrophilic substitution ranked?

By the electron-donating power of the substituents on the ring: strongest donors give the fastest reaction.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Counting π electrons of a ring that has an sp3 carbon.

• Calling a 4nπ planar ring aromatic (it is anti-aromatic).

• Moving atoms (not only electrons) between resonance structures.

• Applying the electrophilic substitution order to nucleophilic substitution.

2. Formulae and laws

Carbocation stability

benzyl, allyl >3∘>2∘>1∘>CH3+

Radical stability

3∘>2∘>1∘>CH3⋅ (benzyl and allyl radicals are very stable)

Carbanion stability

CH3−>1∘>2∘>3∘ (opposite to carbocations); more s-character stabilises

Hyperconjugation structures

number of α C-H bonds: (CH3)3C+ has 9, CH3CH2+ has 3

Hydride affinity

the more stable the carbocation, the lower its hydride affinity

Resonance and lone pairs

−OMe ortho/para to the cationic centre stabilises it strongly

3. Concepts and skills

How do I compare carbocations?

Check for resonance first (benzylic, allylic, adjacent lone pair), then count α-alkyl groups for hyperconjugation and +I effect.

Why does a −NO2 group destabilise a carbocation?

It withdraws electrons, increasing the positive charge on the carbon.

Which carbanion is more stable?

The one on the more electronegative or more s-character carbon, or next to a −M group such as −NO2, −CN, −C=O.

What is the most stable radical on homolytic cleavage?

The one with the weakest C-H bond being broken: benzylic, allylic, then tertiary.

How are cations compared when one has more resonance forms?

The cation with more equivalent resonance structures is more stable.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Applying the carbocation order to carbanions.

• Ignoring resonance when an alkyl-substituted cation competes with a benzylic one.

• Counting hyperconjugation of C-C bonds instead of α C-H bonds.

• Forgetting that a +M group in the meta position gives no resonance stabilisation.

3. Concepts and skills

Why is the extract boiled with HNO3 before adding AgNO3?

To destroy NaCN and Na2S, which would also give precipitates.

Which nitrogen compounds do not give Lassaigne's test?

Those without carbon (hydrazine, N2H4) cannot form NaCN; diazonium salts lose nitrogen on heating.

What colour change identifies each element?

N: Prussian blue; S: purple or black; Cl: white precipitate; Br: pale yellow; I: yellow; N+S: blood red.

Why do we use the sodium extract?

It converts covalently bound elements into ionic compounds that can be detected by ordinary tests.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Mixing up the sulphur and nitrogen test colours.

• Forgetting the acid step before AgNO3.

• Assuming every nitrogen compound gives the test (hydrazine does not).

• Writing FeCl3 for the red colour instead of Fe(SCN)2+.

1. How do I spot this type?

• A statement or a list of compounds asks which effect is shown (−I, +M, hyperconjugation).

• The shortest bond, the strongest dipole, or the set of meta directing groups is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Inductive effect

permanent; through σ bonds; falls rapidly with distance

Resonance (mesomeric) effect

permanent; through π bonds and lone pairs; +M: −OH,−NH2; −M: −NO2,−C=O

Electromeric effect

temporary; only in the presence of an attacking reagent

Ortho/para directing

−OH,−OR,−NH2,−NHCOCH3,−R,−X

Meta directing

−NO2,−CN,−CHO,−COOH,−SO3H,−COR

Bond length

a bond with double-bond character from resonance is shorter than a pure single bond

3. Concepts and skills

What is the difference between inductive and mesomeric effects?

Inductive shifts σ electrons along the chain; mesomeric delocalises π electrons or lone pairs through conjugation.

Why are halogens ortho/para directing but deactivating?

Their +M effect directs, but their strong −I effect decreases the ring's reactivity.

How is the C-Cl bond length compared?

Resonance with an adjacent π bond gives the C-Cl bond some double-bond character, shortening it.

Which effect is responsible for the stability of alkenes with more alkyl groups?

Hyperconjugation: delocalisation of σ C-H electrons into the adjacent π orbital.

How do I find the number of hyperconjugation structures?

Count the α hydrogens on carbons attached to the sp​2 carbon or cation.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Calling the electromeric effect permanent.

• Treating −OH as meta directing.

• Ignoring that halogens are deactivating even though they direct to ortho/para.

• Counting hyperconjugation from carbons that carry no hydrogen.

2. Formulae and laws

Nucleophile

electron-pair donor: OH−,CN−,NH3,H2O,ROH

Electrophile

electron-pair acceptor: H+,NO2+,BF3,AlCl3,R+

Nucleophilicity along a period

NH2−>OH−>F− (less electronegative is stronger)

Anion versus neutral

RO−>ROH; NH2−>NH3

Ambident nucleophiles

CN− (C or N), NO2− (N or O): KCN gives R-CN, AgCN gives R-NC

3. Concepts and skills

How do I identify a nucleophile?

It has a lone pair, a negative charge or a π bond that can donate electrons.

How does solvent affect nucleophilicity?

In protic solvents small anions (F​−) are strongly solvated and less nucleophilic; large anions (I​−) are better nucleophiles.

Which are electrophilic centres in a molecule?

Carbons attached to electronegative atoms or in carbonyl groups, and electron-deficient atoms.

Why do KCN and AgCN give different products?

KCN is ionic and attacks through carbon; AgCN is covalent and attacks through nitrogen.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Counting H3O+ or BF3 as nucleophiles.

• Ordering nucleophilicity by basicity in a protic solvent without considering size.

• Treating a neutral molecule with a lone pair as a non-nucleophile.

• Missing that an alkene is a nucleophile (it donates π electrons).
