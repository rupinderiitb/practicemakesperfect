How to use these notes

1. Do these pages BEFORE the practice sheet, one type at a time.

2. Close all books and notes. Fill the blanks from memory; leave a gap if you cannot recall.

3. Then press “Show completed notes” under that type, correct your answers in a different colour (or your own notes), and note what you missed.

4. Now do that type's questions on the sheet. Come back to the notes after the sheet to add anything new.

5. Re-fill the same page a week later with the prompts covered – this is what makes it stick.

Typing in the notes boxes
  • Your answers are saved on this device only. Use “Save my notes to a file” to keep a copy or move to another device.
  • Tap a box and use the bar at the bottom of the screen.
  • Subscript or superscript: select the text and press x₂ or xⁿ, or press the button, type, and press it again.
  • Back to normal text: press Aa, or press the right arrow at the end of the subscript or superscript.
  • Fraction: press a/b, type the top, press Tab, type the bottom, press Tab again to continue.
  • You can also just type 1/2 or sqrt(x).
Keyboard shortcuts (computer keyboard)
  • Ctrl+H: superscript
  • Ctrl+L: subscript
  • Ctrl+Space: back to normal text
  • Ctrl+F: fraction (Tab moves from top to bottom to out)
  • Ctrl+I: integral ∫
  • Ctrl+S: sigma Σ
  • Ctrl+R: square root √
  • Ctrl+P: pi π
  • Ctrl+E: infinity ∞
  • Ctrl+> (Ctrl+Shift+. on most keyboards): arrow →; press again for the “implies” arrow ⇒
  • These work only while you are typing in a notes box. On a phone use the bar at the bottom.

Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'MX3 (or A2B) is x% ionised; find i' or the reverse, with the answer in the nearest integer.

• A solute that dimerises in benzene or water, or a weak acid with a freezing-point depression given, and the degree of dissociation or Ka is asked.

• A mixture (sea water, Pb(NO3)2 with NaCl, CrCl3⋅xNH3) whose boiling or freezing point is given.

2. Formulae and laws

Dissociation into n ions

i=1+(n−1)α

Dimerisation

i=1−α2

van't Hoff factor from data

i=normal molar massobserved molar mass=ΔTobservedΔTcalculated

Colligative effect with i

ΔTb=iKbm; ΔTf=iKfm; π=iCRT

Weak acid HA

Ka=Cα21−α and i=1+α

Boiling point of the solution

Tb=Tb∘+ΔTb; freezing point Tf=Tf∘−ΔTf

Complex CrCl3⋅xNH3 with coordination number 6

[Cr(NH3)xCl6−x]Clx−3: i=1+(x−3)=x−2 ions

3. Concepts and skills

What does i<1 tell you?

The solute associates, so there are fewer particles than formula units (for example a carboxylic acid dimer in benzene).

How are two solutes in one solution treated?

Find the moles of particles of each, add them, and use ΔT=K× (total particles) ÷ kg of solvent.

Why is i closer to 1 in a concentrated solution?

Ion pairing reduces the effective number of particles; the more dilute the solution, the larger the degree of ionisation.

A solute is 60% ionised: how many mol of particles per mol?

For A2B3 (n=5): i=1+4(0.6)=3.4.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using i=1+α for every salt instead of 1+(n−1)α.

• Forgetting that dimerisation gives i=1−α/2 (not 1−α).

• Giving ΔTb when the boiling point is asked (add it to 373 K or 100∘.

• Using the volume of solvent as if it were the mass, or 0.5 L as 0.5 kg without the density.

Completed notes – Type 2

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'w g of a solute in W g of solvent raises the boiling point by ΔT; find the molar mass' (or Kb, or the mass).

• Two solutions or two solvents are compared and a ratio (of ΔT, K or molar masses) is asked.

• A solution is cooled below its freezing point and the mass of ice that separates is asked, or ΔTf is read from a vapour-pressure graph.

2. Formulae and laws

Elevation and depression

ΔTb=Kbm; ΔTf=Kfm

Molality

m=w/MW(kg of solvent)

Molar mass

M=1000KwΔTW(g)

Constants from the solvent

Kb=RTb2M11000ΔHvap; Kf=RTf2M11000ΔHfus

Ice that separates at a lower temperature

m′=ΔTf′Kf; water left =nsolutem′; ice =W− water left

Same solute, same solvent mass

ΔT∝K and, for equal mass of different solutes, ΔT∝1M

3. Concepts and skills

What is the temperature asked for, ΔT or T?

Read the last line: the freezing point is Tf∘−ΔTf, the boiling point is Tb∘+ΔTb.

How do you handle two compounds (AB and AB2) with different ΔT?

Find M of each from its ΔT; the difference is the atomic mass of B; then find A.

Is the molality changed when the solution is cooled?

No. Molality uses mass, so it is fixed by the composition at that temperature.

On a vapour-pressure graph where is ΔTf?

It is the horizontal gap between the freezing point of the pure solvent and of the solution at the same vapour pressure.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the mass of the solution instead of the mass of the solvent for m.

• Writing ΔTf when asked for the freezing point in kelvin.

• Mixing g and kg in M=1000Kw/(ΔTW).

• Forgetting to convert the volume of the solvent to mass with its density.

3. Concepts and skills

Which terms depend on temperature?

Molarity (it has a volume in it); molality, mole fraction, mass percent and ppm do not.

What basis makes a conversion easy?

Take 1 L of solution, 1 kg of solvent or 100 g of solution, then find the other quantities.

How is a hydrate treated?

The molar mass includes the water of crystallisation, for example CuSO4⋅5H2O=249.5.

Is 'mole' a unit of concentration?

No; it is a unit of amount. Mass percent, mole fraction, molarity, ppm and molality are.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the mass of solution as the mass of solvent for molality.

• Forgetting the density when the volume of the solvent is given.

• Ignoring the water of crystallisation of a hydrate.

• Using mL in place of L in molarity.

• Two solutions are isotonic or mixed and the osmotic pressure of the mixture is asked.

• A membrane, a cell or two chambers are drawn and the direction of osmosis or the condition for reverse osmosis is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Osmotic pressure

π=iCRT=inVRT

Molar mass

M=wRTπV

Isotonic solutions

i1C1=i2C2

Mixing two solutions

πmix=π1V1+π2V2V1+V2

Net osmotic pressure between two solutions

Temperature at constant C

π∝T; R=0.083 L bar K​−1 mol​−1 =0.0821 L atm K​−1 mol​−1

3. Concepts and skills

In which direction does the solvent move?

From the solution of lower iC to the solution of higher iC, through the semipermeable membrane.

What is reverse osmosis?

A pressure greater than π is applied on the concentrated side, so the solvent flows out of it.

What happens to a cell in a hypertonic solution?

It loses water and shrinks.

Why is π used for polymers?

The effect is large even for dilute solutions of large molecules.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using Pa with R in L bar (convert 400 Pa =0.004 bar).

• Taking the volume of the solvent instead of the solution.

• Forgetting i for NaCl or CaCl2.

• Choosing the wrong direction of flow.

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Arrange the solutions in the order of their boiling points' (or freezing points).

• 'Which solution has the lowest freezing point?' with four solutes of the same molarity.

• 'The number of isotonic pairs' from a list of solutions, or a list of statements.

2. Formulae and laws

Colligative effect

∝i×C (or i×m)

Ions per formula unit (complete dissociation)

NaCl:2; CaCl2:3; AlCl3:4; Al2(SO4)3:5; K4[Fe(CN)6]:5; urea, glucose: 1

Boiling point order

larger iC gives a higher Tb, a lower Tf and a lower vapour pressure

Isotonic

equal iC (equal osmotic pressure)

Relative lowering of vapour pressure

∝ moles of solute (the same mass of solvent): smaller molar mass gives a larger lowering

3. Concepts and skills

Which has the lowest freezing point: 0.06 M Al2(SO4)3 or glucose?

Al2(SO4)3 (i=5, iC=0.30) against 0.06 for glucose.

Why does acetic acid in benzene give a smaller effect?

It dimerises in benzene, so the number of particles falls.

How do you count a double salt such as KCl⋅MgCl2⋅6H2O?

K+, Mg2+ and 3Cl−: 5 particles.

Is the freezing point of a dilute NaCl solution lower than that of urea of the same molarity?

Yes: iC is twice as large.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Comparing molarities without multiplying by i.

• Counting K4[Fe(CN)6] as 4 ions instead of 5.

• Reversing the order for freezing and boiling points.

• Treating glycine or hydrazine as strongly ionised.

Completed notes – Type 6

1. How do I spot this type?

• Two liquids A and B with given p∘ and a given composition; the vapour pressure or the vapour mole fraction is asked.

• The total pressure is given at two compositions and pA∘ is asked.

• A comparison of xA/xB with yA/yB or a plot of 1/x against 1/y.

2. Formulae and laws

Partial pressure

pA=xApA∘

Total pressure

P=xApA∘+xBpB∘=pB∘+(pA∘−pB∘)xA

Mole fraction in the vapour

yA=pAP=xApA∘P

Ratio

yAyB=xAxB⋅pA∘pB∘

Plot of 1/y1 against 1/x1

slope p2∘p1∘, intercept p1∘−p2∘p1∘

3. Concepts and skills

Which component is richer in the vapour?

The more volatile one (higher p∘), so yA/yB>xA/xB if pA∘>pB∘.

What is ideal behaviour?

ΔHmix=0 and ΔVmix=0; A–B forces equal A–A and B–B forces.

How do you find pA∘ from two total pressures?

Write P=xApA∘+xBpB∘ for both compositions and solve the two equations.

Which component is least volatile?

The one with the smaller p∘.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the mole fraction in the liquid as the mole fraction in the vapour.

• Using p∘ for the total pressure.

• Swapping xA and xB when the question says 'xB=0.5'.

• Not dividing by the total pressure to get y.

Raoult's law for a non-volatile solute

p∘−pp∘=xsolute=nn+N

Dilute solution

p∘−pp∘≈nN=w/MW/M1

Electrolyte

replace n by in

Vapour pressure of the solution

3. Concepts and skills

What is relative lowering?

The fraction (p∘−p)/p∘, which equals the mole fraction of the solute and does not depend on the nature of the solute.

If the vapour pressure is reduced to half, what is xsolute?

0.5, so nsolute=nsolvent.

What happens in two open beakers (solvent and solution) in a closed container?

The solvent evaporates and condenses in the solution: the solution volume increases.

Why is the lowering the same for equal moles?

It depends only on the number of solute particles.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using n/N when the solute is a large fraction (use n/(n+N)).

• Forgetting i for an electrolyte.

• Using mass instead of moles.

• Taking p instead of p∘−p as the lowering.

Completed notes – Type 8

1. How do I spot this type?

• A gas at a given partial pressure over water and KH is given; the moles or mass dissolved per litre is asked.

• A table of KH for several gases or a statement about KH.

• A graph of KH against temperature or of partial pressure against mole fraction.

2. Formulae and laws

Henry's law

p=KHx, so x=pKH

Moles per litre of water

n≈x×55.5 (for 1 kg of water)

A 55.5 molal solution

x≈0.5, so p=0.5KH

Higher KH

lower solubility at the same pressure

3. Concepts and skills

How does KH change with temperature?

It rises for most gases in water, so solubility falls on heating.

Is the solubility proportional to p?

Yes, at constant temperature (for a gas that does not react).

Why is x small for gases?

KH is large (kbar), so x is of the order 10−5 and n/(n+N)≈n/N.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using kPa and kbar together without converting.

• Forgetting the 55.5 mol per kg of water.

• Taking a higher KH to mean a higher solubility.

• Mixing up the order of KH for the gases in a graph.

Completed notes – Type 9

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Which mixture shows a positive (or negative) deviation from Raoult's law?'

• A match of mixtures with the type of azeotrope, or an odd one out.

• A vapour-pressure curve is drawn and the boiling point or the strength of attraction is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Positive deviation

p>pRaoult; ΔHmix>0; ΔVmix>0; minimum-boiling azeotrope

Negative deviation

p<pRaoult; ΔHmix<0; ΔVmix<0; maximum-boiling azeotrope

Boiling point on a curve

the temperature at which the vapour pressure equals the external pressure (1 atm)

3. Concepts and skills

Examples of positive deviation

ethanol–water, acetone–CS2, methanol–CHCl3: weaker A–B forces.

Examples of negative deviation

chloroform–acetone, phenol–aniline, HNO3–water: stronger A–B forces.

What does a higher vapour pressure at the same temperature mean?

Weaker intermolecular attractions in that liquid.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Confusing a minimum-boiling azeotrope with negative deviation.

• Reading the boiling point at the wrong pressure.

• Taking a higher vapour pressure to mean stronger attractions.
