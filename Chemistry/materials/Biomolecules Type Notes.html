Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• The words maltose, lactose, sucrose, starch, cellulose, glycogen, amylose or amylopectin appear in the stem.

• 'Glycosidic linkage between C1 of ... and C4 of ...' or 'two monomers of ...' is asked.

• A statement or assertion–reason question marked 'incorrect' or 'not true' about one sugar, often with a structure drawn.

2. Formulae and laws

Maltose

α-D-glucose + α-D-glucose, C1−C4 (α); reducing

Lactose

β-D-galactose + β-D-glucose, C1−C4 (β); reducing

Sucrose

α-D-glucose + β-D-fructose, C1−C2; non-reducing; invert sugar on hydrolysis

Amylose, amylopectin

α(1→4) chain; amylopectin has α(1→6) branches too

Cellulose

β(1→4) glucose, unbranched, not digested by humans

Glycogen

animal starch, highly branched α(1→4) and α(1→6)

3. Concepts and skills

Why is sucrose non-reducing?

Both anomeric carbons (glucose C1 and fructose C2) are in the glycosidic bond, so no free hemiacetal exists.

What is invert sugar?

The equimolar glucose–fructose mixture from sucrose; it is laevorotatory because fructose (−92°) outweighs glucose (+52°).

How do you recognise the α-anomer of a disaccharide drawn as a ring?

Look at the free anomeric OH and the glycosidic O: both down (opposite to CH2OH) is α.

Which enzymes act on which sugar?

Invertase on sucrose, maltase on maltose, zymase on glucose (to ethanol), amylase on starch.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Writing the sucrose linkage as C1−C1 or calling sucrose reducing.

• Calling lactose α-linked (it is β).

• Calling amylose branched or amylopectin unbranched.

• Treating invert sugar as another name for sucrose.

Completed notes – Type 2

1. How do I spot this type?

• A Fischer projection (CHO at the top, OH on the right or left) or a Haworth ring is shown and the name, the D/L label or the correct structure is asked.

• 'Glucose with Br2 water, HNO3, HI, acetic anhydride' is given in a list to be matched.

• Molisch, Barfoed, Seliwanoff, Fehling or Tollens appears in the options.

2. Formulae and laws

D/L

D: OH on the right at the last stereocentre; L: mirror image (every centre inverted)

Haworth

right in Fischer = down; left = up; D: CH2OH up; α: anomeric OH down, β: up

Stereocentres of glucose

4 (open chain), 5 (ring); chiral carbons in sucrose: 9

Glucose + Br2/H2O

gluconic acid (CHO → COOH)

Glucose + HNO3

saccharic acid (CHO and CH2OH both oxidised)

Glucose + HI, Δ

n-hexane; with acetic anhydride, the pentaacetate (5 OH)

3. Concepts and skills

Why does fructose reduce Tollens' reagent although it has no CHO?

In base it forms an enediol and rearranges to glucose and mannose, which have the aldehyde.

Why does glucose pentaacetate not form an oxime?

The ring (hemiacetal) form has no free CHO and all OH groups are acetylated.

What are epimers and anomers?

Epimers differ at one carbon other than C1 (glucose/galactose at C4); anomers differ only at C1 (α/β glucose).

Which test separates a ketose?

Seliwanoff (resorcinol/HCl): ketoses give red colour quickly.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Reading Fischer right/left as Haworth up/down the wrong way round.

• Counting the ring oxygen's carbon wrongly when naming pyranose and furanose.

• Using Barfoed's test for a disaccharide (it detects monosaccharides).

• Saying glucose gives Schiff's test (it does not).

Completed notes – Type 3

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Primary / secondary / tertiary / quaternary' appears in the options, or α-helix and β-pleated sheet.

• A heating, boiling or egg-coagulation situation is described.

• A positive test (Biuret, ninhydrin, Xanthoproteic, carbylamine) is asked for a list of compounds.

2. Formulae and laws

Primary

amino acid sequence; peptide bonds; survives denaturation

Secondary

α-helix, β-sheet; hydrogen bonds

Tertiary

folding; disulphide, ionic, hydrogen, van der Waals

Biuret

violet with Cu2+/OH−: at least two peptide bonds

Ninhydrin

purple (Ruhemann's purple) with free α-amino acids and proteins

Xanthoproteic

yellow-orange with aromatic amino acids (Tyr, Phe, Trp)

3. Concepts and skills

What does denaturation change?

Secondary, tertiary (and quaternary) structure; the primary structure remains.

Fibrous or globular?

Fibrous (keratin, collagen, myosin) is insoluble; globular (albumin, enzymes) is water soluble.

Does a dipeptide give the Biuret test?

No: it has one peptide bond; a tripeptide does.

Which peptide gives carbylamine and ceric ammonium nitrate tests?

One with a free primary amine and an alcoholic OH (for example Ser–Lys).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Saying denaturation breaks peptide bonds.

• Assigning the α-helix to the tertiary structure.

• Using Biuret for a single amino acid or a dipeptide.

• Confusing the Barfoed test with a test for amino acids.

Completed notes – Type 4

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Essential', 'basic', 'sulphur containing' or a one-letter code is asked.

• The structure of an amino acid (tyrosine, histidine, valine) is to be chosen, often at a stated pH.

• A count of chiral centres, carbon atoms or negative charges is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Essential

Val, Leu, Ile, Lys, Thr, Met, Phe, Trp, His, Arg

Codes

R Arg, D Asp, N Asn, A Ala, E Glu, Q Gln, Y Tyr, W Trp

Zwitterion

H3N+−CHR−COO− at the isoelectric point

Charge by pH

acidic pH: cation; basic pH: anion

Chirality

all except glycine have a chiral α-carbon; Ile and Thr have two

3. Concepts and skills

Why do amino acids have high melting points and dissolve in water but not in benzene?

They exist as zwitterions, which are ionic.

Which amino acid has only one basic group?

Asparagine: the amide is not basic; Lys, Arg, His have a second basic group.

Which amino acids contain sulphur?

Cysteine and methionine; cysteine dimerises to cystine through the SH group.

How many negative charges at pH 12.5 for Gly–Glu–Asp–Tyr?

Three COO⁻ (C-terminal, Glu, Asp) and the phenoxide of Tyr: 4.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Counting arginine as non-essential (NCERT lists it as essential).

• Forgetting the side-chain COOH of Asp and Glu when counting charges.

• Drawing the cation instead of the zwitterion at the isoelectric point.

• Confusing the one-letter codes of Asp (D), Asn (N), Glu (E), Gln (Q).

• A peptide drawn or named (Gly–Ala, Ile–Arg–Pro) with a count of sp2 or chiral carbons.

2. Formulae and laws

Peptide bonds

n amino acids, linear: n−1; cyclic: n

Sequences

n different amino acids used once: n!

Tripeptides from k amino acids

k3 (repeats allowed)

Minimum molar mass

M=Mamino acidmass fraction

Osmotic pressure

π=CRT

3. Concepts and skills

How is a peptide written?

N-terminal residue on the left, C-terminal on the right; the bond joins its COOH to the next NH₂.

What does excess acetic anhydride do to Asn–Ser?

It acetylates the free NH₂ and the OH of serine, not the amide.

How are sp² carbons counted in a peptide?

Each C=O (one per peptide bond and the terminal COOH) plus every aromatic ring carbon.

How do you get the structure from hydrolysis products?

The product that gives lactic acid with HNO₂ is alanine; the one that forms a diketopiperazine on heating is glycine.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using n instead of n−1 for peptide bonds.

• Forgetting that Ile, Thr have two chiral centres.

• Using 57 or 75 for a glycine residue inconsistently.

Completed notes – Type 6

1. How do I spot this type?

• A base (adenine, thymine, cytosine, uracil) is drawn and named, or one drawn base is 'incorrect'.

• A strand sequence is given and the complementary strand or the number of hydrogen bonds is asked.

• A statement about DNA and RNA with 'which one is found in RNA' or 'sugar moiety'.

2. Formulae and laws

Pairing

A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds); in RNA A=U

Pyrimidines

C, T, U (one ring); purines: A, G (two rings)

Nucleotide

base + sugar + phosphate

Sugars

DNA: β-D-2-deoxyribose; RNA: β-D-ribose

3. Concepts and skills

How are two nucleotides joined?

By a phosphodiester linkage between the 3′ and 5′ positions.

How are the hydrogen bonds of a strand counted?

Write the complementary strand, then 2 per A–T pair and 3 per G–C pair.

How many oxygen atoms in the uracil nucleotide?

2 (uracil) + 4 (ribose) + 3 (extra phosphate oxygens) = 9.

Which base is only in RNA?

Uracil; thymine is only in DNA.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Pairing A with U in DNA.

• Calling RNA double-stranded.

• Taking the 5′-strand as written for the 3′ end.

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Fat soluble', 'stored in the body' or 'not excreted easily' appears.

• A match of vitamin (thiamine, riboflavin, ascorbic acid, pyridoxine) with a disease is given.

• 'Vitamin B12' with cobalt or a vitamin C structure is shown.

2. Formulae and laws

Fat soluble

A, D, E, K (stored)

Water soluble

B group and C (excreted), except B12 (stored)

Deficiency

A: xerophthalmia; B1: beriberi; B2: cheilosis; B6: convulsions; C: scurvy; D: rickets; K: slow clotting

Metal

B12: cobalt

3. Concepts and skills

Which vitamins can be stored?

A, D, E, K and B12 (liver); the other water-soluble ones are excreted.

Which vitamin is an acid?

Vitamin C, ascorbic acid.

What does vitamin K do?

It helps blood clotting; deficiency increases clotting time.

Which vitamin C structure is most stable?

The enediol lactone in which the C=C is conjugated with the C=O.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Calling B12 non-storable.

• Mixing riboflavin (cheilosis) and thiamine (beriberi).

• Writing that vitamin K decreases clotting time.
