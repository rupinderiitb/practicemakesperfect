Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'The solubility product of ... is ...; find the solubility' (or the reverse) for a salt such as PbS, Bi2S3, Ca(OH)2, Cr(OH)3 or Zr3(PO4)4.

• The solubility is given in g/L or g per 100 mL (for example AgCl, CaF2) and Ksp or −logKsp is asked.

• An order of Ksp or of solubility for different salts, or a solubility curve of a salt.

2. Formulae and laws

Dissociation of AxBy and its Ksp

AxBy⇌xAy++yBx−; Ksp=(xs)x(ys)y=xxyysx+y

AB (1:1 salt)

Ksp=s2

AB2 or A2B (for example Ca(OH)2, CaF2, Ag2CO3)

Ksp=4s3

AB3 (for example Cr(OH)3, Al(OH)3)

Ksp=27s4

A2B3 or A3B2 (for example Bi2S3, Ca3(PO4)2)

Ksp=108s5

A3B4 (for example Zr3(PO4)4)

Ksp=3344s7=6912s7, so s=(Ksp6912)1/7

Molar solubility from mass solubility

s(mol/L)=g per litreM; for W g per 100 mL, s=10WM

[OH−] in a saturated solution of M(OH)3

[OH−]=3s=(3Ksp)1/4

Comparing two salts

Same formula type: larger Ksp means larger s. Different types: calculate s for each

3. Concepts and skills

Why can you not compare Ksp of AgBr and Ca(OH)2 directly to compare their solubilities?

Their Ksp expressions have different powers of s (s2 and 4s3). Convert each to s first.

What does 'sparingly soluble' tell you?

The dissolved amount is small, so s is small and Ksp is used; ions from the dissolved part are fully dissociated.

How do you handle a solubility given as x g per 100 mL?

Multiply by 10 to get g/L, then divide by the molar mass to get mol/L.

What does the curve of [Y] against [X] for a saturated salt show?

Every point has [X]a[Y]b=Ksp; read one point and find Ksp and the formula.

How is the order of Ksp for Ca(OH)2, AgBr, PbS, HgS found?

From solubility: the hydroxide is far more soluble than the halide, and the sulphides are the least soluble (HgS is the smallest).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using Ksp=s2 for every salt.

• Forgetting the coefficient inside the bracket, for example [F−]=2s (not s).

• Using g per 100 mL as if it were g/L.

• Taking the wrong root (square root for a 4s3 salt).

• Comparing salts of different types by Ksp alone.

Completed notes – Type 2

1. How do I spot this type?

• A weak acid and its salt (or a weak base and its salt) are mixed and the pH, the ratio of salt to acid or the amount of salt is asked.

• Strong acid (for example HCl) is added to a buffer of NH3 and NH4Cl and the change in pH is asked.

• A weak acid or base is titrated part of the way ('when 25 mL of NaOH has been added'), or a weak base is mixed with an acid in less than the equivalent amount.

2. Formulae and laws

Acidic buffer; basic buffer

pH=pKa+log[salt][acid]; pOH=pKb+log[salt][base]

The two constants

pKa+pKb=14 for a conjugate pair; pH+pOH=14

With moles (the volume cancels when both are in the same solution)

pH=pKa+lognsaltnacid

Adding n mol of a strong acid to NH3 / NH4Cl

nNH3→nNH3−n and nNH4+→nNH4++n

Half-neutralisation of a weak acid

[salt]=[acid], so pH=pKa

Partial neutralisation: mole table

Moles of acid left =nacid−nbase; moles of salt formed =nbase (base limiting)

Excess weak base left after adding a strong acid

pOH=pKb+lognNH4+nNH4OH

Buffer capacity is largest when

[salt]=[acid], that is pH=pKa; useful range pKa±1

log values to remember

log2=0.30, log3=0.48, log5=0.70

3. Concepts and skills

What makes a solution a buffer?

A weak acid with its conjugate base (or a weak base with its conjugate acid) in comparable amounts; it resists change of pH on adding small amounts of acid or base.

Is 'a salt and an acid mixed in any quantities' a buffer?

No. The two must be a conjugate pair in comparable amounts; a statement saying 'any quantities' is wrong. Blood is a natural buffer.

How do you treat H2SO4 added to NH4OH?

Count 2 mol H+ per mol of H2SO4, subtract from the moles of base, and apply the base-buffer formula with NH4+ formed.

When the acid exactly neutralises the weak base, what then?

Only the salt is left, so use salt hydrolysis (Type 4), not Henderson.

How is the amount of salt for a given pH found?

Rearrange: [salt][acid]=10pH−pKa, then multiply by the known concentration (and by the molar mass for grams).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the initial moles instead of the moles after the reaction.

• Putting the acid over the salt in Henderson's equation.

• Using pH=pKb+… for a base buffer (it gives pOH).

• Forgetting that H2SO4 gives 2 H+.

• Treating a solution with only salt left as a buffer.

• A strong acid or base of given concentration is diluted (with an equal volume of water, or to change the pH from 1 to 2) and the pH or volume is asked.

• Two solutions (HCl with H2SO4, or NaOH with H2SO4) are mixed and the pH is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Definitions

pH=−log[H+]; pOH=−log[OH−]; pH+pOH=14 (at 25∘

Ionic product of water

Kw=[H+][OH−]=10−14 at 25∘C

Dilution

M1V1=M2V2; a 10-fold dilution of a strong acid raises pH by 1

Change in [H+] by a factor f

ΔpH=logf (for example ×1000 gives 3 units)

Hydroxides giving 2 OH−

Ba(OH)2, Ca(OH)2, Mg(OH)2: [OH−]=2× concentration (when complete dissociation is assumed)

Mixing an acid and a base

nH+=basicity×M×V; [H+]excess=nH+−nOH−Vtotal

Mixing two acids

3. Concepts and skills

Why is the pH of 10−8 M HCl not 8?

Water contributes 10−7 M H+; the pH is just below 7 (≈6.98).

How do you mix an acid and a base quickly?

Compare the moles of H+ and OH−: the larger decides acid or base, and its excess goes into the total volume.

When equal volumes are mixed, what happens to the concentrations?

Each is halved (the volume doubles).

What does pH do when [H+] is increased by 1000 times?

It decreases by 3 units.

If NaOH is added to HCl and the product is poured into a larger flask, what is checked first?

Whether the mixture is neutral, acidic or basic from the mole count; then dilute for the final concentration.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the concentration of H2SO4 as the [H+] (it is twice).

• Dividing by the volume of one solution after mixing.

• Forgetting that Ba(OH)2 gives 2OH−.

• Forgetting to dilute the volume after mixing.

• Reporting pH from [OH−] without 14−pOH.

Completed notes – Type 4

1. How do I spot this type?

• The pH, degree of dissociation or Ka of a weak acid (or the Kb of a base such as ethylamine) is asked from the concentration and the pH.

• A weak acid is dissolved in a strong acid (common ion), or diluted, and the new degree of dissociation or pH is asked.

• The pH or the nature (acidic, basic, neutral) of a salt solution such as NH4Cl, CH3COONa, ammonium acetate or calcium lactate.

2. Formulae and laws

Ostwald's dilution law

Ka=Cα21−α≈Cα2 so α=KaC (if α<5%)

Weak acid; weak base

[H+]=KaC, pH=1/2(pKa−logC); [OH−]=KbC

Weak acid in a strong acid of concentration c (common ion)

α≈Kac (because [H+]≈c)

Conjugate pair

Ka×Kb=Kw; pKa+pKb=14

Consecutive ionisations

Koverall=Ka1×Ka2 (not the sum)

Salt of weak acid and strong base (for example CH3COONa)

pH=7+1/2pKa+1/2logC

Salt of strong acid and weak base (for example NH4Cl)

pH=7−1/2pKb−1/2logC

Salt of weak acid and weak base (for example ammonium acetate)

pH=7+1/2(pKa−pKb), independent of C

Degree of dissociation from the pH

α=[H+]C with [H+]=10−pH

3. Concepts and skills

When is the approximation 1−α≈1 not allowed?

When α is more than a few per cent (large Ka or very dilute solution); then solve the quadratic.

What happens to the degree of dissociation on dilution?

It increases, although [H+] decreases.

What is the pH of a salt of a weak acid and a weak base with Ka=Kb?

7.

Why does a solution of (NH4)2CO3 turn basic?

The carbonate hydrolyses more strongly (Kb of CO32−) than the ammonium (Ka of NH4+); compare Ka and Kb.

How do you find the concentration of the salt ion for Ca(lactate)2?

Two lactate ions per formula unit: [lactate]=2× the salt concentration.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using α from Ka/C when the acid is in a strong acid solution.

• Adding Ka1 and Ka2 instead of multiplying.

• Using the formula for a strong acid on a weak one.

• Forgetting the second lactate or acetate ion when the salt gives more than one.

• Reporting pOH as pH.

1. How do I spot this type?

• The solubility of a salt in a solution that already contains one of its ions (CdSO4 in H2SO4, BaSO4 in K2SO4, PbI2 in Pb(NO3)2).

• The solubility of a hydroxide in NaOH or in a buffer of given pH (Zn(OH)2, Al(OH)3, Cd(OH)2).

2. Formulae and laws

Solubility with a common ion of concentration c

s=Ksp[common ion]n (with the other ion's coefficient kept)

PbI2 in Pb(NO3)2 (c M)

Ksp=c(2s)2, so s=Ksp4c

Ag2CO3 in AgNO3 (c M)

Ksp=c2s, so s=Kspc2

M(OH)n in a medium of fixed [OH−]

s=Ksp[OH−]n with [OH−]=10pH−14

Salt of a weak acid (AgCN) at a given [H+]

s2=Ksp(1+[H+]Ka)

Solubility order in AgCl questions

Pure water > solutions with Cl− or Ag+; more common ion means less solubility

Check of the approximation

s≪ the common-ion concentration, so [common]+s≈[common]

3. Concepts and skills

Why does the solubility decrease with a common ion?

Le Chatelier's principle: the extra ion shifts the dissolution equilibrium towards the solid.

Which gives the lowest solubility of AgCl: 0.01 M HCl, 0.01 M KCl, water or 0.01 M AgNO3?

The salts with a common ion (Cl− or Ag+) reduce it; water gives the highest.

Why is a salt of a weak acid more soluble in acid?

The anion is removed as the weak acid (CN−+H+→HCN), which shifts the equilibrium to dissolve more.

How does the buffer help in a hydroxide question?

It fixes [OH−], so the solubility equals Ksp/[OH−]n with no other terms.

Can you ignore s against the common-ion concentration?

Yes when s is very small (check after you find it).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using pure-water s in the formula for the common-ion case.

• Forgetting the 2 in [I−]=2s for PbI2.

• Squaring the common ion for a 1:1 salt.

• Using pOH where [OH−] is needed (or the reverse).

• Answering the order of solubility from Ksp without using the common ion.

Completed notes – Type 6

1. How do I spot this type?

• Two salt solutions are mixed and 'will a precipitate form?' (PbCl2, BaF2, AY2) or the mass of the precipitate is asked.

• A base is added gradually to a solution with two metal ions (A2+ and B3+) and 'which precipitates first' is asked.

• 'The pH at which Mg(OH)2 begins to precipitate', or AgNO3 added to Cl− and CrO42−.

2. Formulae and laws

Condition for precipitation

Qsp>Ksp: precipitate; Qsp=Ksp: just saturated; Qsp<Ksp: no precipitate

Concentration after mixing

cnew=cVVtotal (equal volumes: halve each)

Qsp for AB2

Qsp=[A2+][B−]2 with the new concentrations

Hydroxide just starts to precipitate: M(OH)2; M(OH)3

[OH−]=Ksp[M2+]; [OH−]=Ksp[M3+]3

Fractional precipitation by Ag+

[Ag+] for AgCl=Ksp[Cl−]; for Ag2CrO4=Ksp[CrO42−]. The smaller value precipitates first

Mass of precipitate (Ca(OH)​2 with Na2SO4)

Limiting reagent in moles × molar mass; then the ion left in solution from the mole table

Common ion by gas (HCl into saturated BaCl2)

[Cl−] rises, Q>Ksp, BaCl2 separates out

3. Concepts and skills

What is the first step in a mixing question?

Find the new concentrations in the total volume, then calculate Qsp.

If two metal hydroxides are possible, which precipitates first?

The one that needs the smaller [OH−] (compare [OH−] required for each).

Can you compare the Ksp values directly for fractional precipitation?

Only for salts of the same type. Otherwise calculate the ion concentration required.

What does Qsp/Ksp equal at the point of precipitation?

1; if the ratio is more than 1, the solution is supersaturated.

How is a leftover ion concentration found after precipitation?

Take the moles that remain after the reaction and divide by the volume.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the initial concentrations instead of the mixed ones.

• Forgetting the power on the concentration in Qsp.

• Comparing Ksp of unlike salts to decide which precipitates first.

• Taking the pH at which the hydroxide is complete instead of when it begins.

• Mixing up Qsp and Ksp (the direction of the inequality).

Methyl orange

3. Concepts and skills

Which form of methyl orange is present at the end point of a base–acid titration?

The red (acid) form, called quinonoid.

Why is phenolphthalein unsuitable for a weak base with a strong acid?

The equivalence point is acidic (pH below 7), outside its range 8.3–10; methyl orange is used.

How can you tell a weak acid from a strong acid on a titration curve?

The weak acid starts at a higher pH, shows a buffer region and ends at a pH above 7.

In the curve of pH against volume of acid added to NaOH, in which direction does the pH move?

Down: from about 13 to about 1, with a sharp fall at the end point.

What is the convention for burette readings?

Ignore the early, scattered reading and use the concordant ones.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the average of all readings including the non-concordant one.

• Confusing which indicator suits which type of titration.

• Forgetting that Na2CO3 needs 2 HCl at the full end point.

• Choosing the curve that rises when acid is added to a base.

• Giving the colour of an indicator in the wrong medium.

3. Concepts and skills

Why can PCl5 not act as a Lewis base?

Phosphorus has no lone pair in PCl5 (NF3, SF4 and ClF3 have lone pairs).

Does the pH of water at 80∘C being below 7 mean it is acidic?

No. It is neutral because [H+]=[OH−]; the neutral pH is lower than 7.

How is amphoteric behaviour of water explained?

Water accepts a proton from an acid (H2S) and donates one to a base (NH3): the Brønsted–Lowry theory, not the Lewis theory.

Why is the pH of 10−8 M HCl not 8?

Acid never gives a basic solution; the water adds 10−7 M H+, so pH is about 6.98.

How do you pick the strongest Brønsted base from a set of structures (aniline, diphenylamine, pyrrole, pyrrolidine)?

Find the one whose lone pair is most available: it is not delocalised (pyrrolidine, an alkyl amine).

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Saying that the pH of hot water is 7 because it is neutral.

• Counting the pH of very dilute acid as above 7.

• Using the Lewis theory to explain the proton transfer of water.

• Confusing conjugate acid and conjugate base.

• Counting only bonded electrons as 'non-bonded'.
